Build a demo showing how a community bank can win back SMB deposits AND lending relationships by becoming the payment facilitator for the software small businesses already use to run their day-to-day operations, instead of sitting behind a third-party processor at arm's length.

**Builder context (reflect this in the README's product-thinking section, don't just leave it here):** this concept comes from someone with 15+ years of direct payments and banking product experience — enterprise payments and real-time rails product leadership at a top-10 US bank, embedded BaaS and multi-issuer card programs at a major payments company, product leadership at a hypergrowth payments gateway — and currently advises vertical SaaS platforms and PE-backed operators on embedded payments and PayFac-as-a-Service models as an independent practice. This isn't a hypothetical fintech idea picked for the exercise — it's the exact problem space the builder works in professionally. The README should say this plainly and specifically, not vaguely gesture at "payments background."

**The problem:** Community banks and credit unions are losing SMB deposits to fintechs and vertical SaaS platforms that make it easy to move money without ever touching a bank relationship. Banks want the deposit relationship back, but don't want the overhead of owning the software experience directly. The usual fix routes payments through a third-party payment facilitator sitting between the SaaS platform and the bank, which means the bank is still an arm's-length sponsor and still doesn't fully trust a ledger it doesn't own. The stronger fix: the bank itself becomes the payment facilitator, with a connector layer letting the SaaS platform plug directly into the bank's own PayFac-as-a-Service capability. Funds never leave the bank's own platform. The SaaS platform's transaction record becomes a real-time view into the bank's own ledger, not a separate system to reconcile after the fact. And because the bank is already the system of record for payments, it's a natural extension for it to also offer point-of-sale financing on large-ticket transactions through the same connector — turning a single embedded checkout moment into both a deposit and a lending relationship.

**The entities:**
- **ABC Community Bank** — fictional community bank/credit union offering PayFac-as-a-Service and embedded point-of-sale financing through a Nymbus-built connector layer.
- **AirConPro** — a fictional SaaS platform (job management, invoicing, scheduling software) used by HVAC contractors. AirConPro is the software vendor; it integrates the Nymbus connector once, and every HVAC contractor using AirConPro inherits the embedded payments/financing experience.
- **CoolAir HVAC** — a fictional HVAC contractor business, one of AirConPro's customers. CoolAir HVAC is the sub-merchant that onboards through the bank's PayFac program and processes the $8,000 transaction in the demo scenario.
- **Mr. Arnold Freeze** — the fictional homeowner/end consumer getting the new AC unit installed. Use his name at the checkout moment and on the resulting transaction/ledger records so the demo reads as a real scenario, not a generic "Customer #1."

**The concept:** AirConPro plugs into ABC Community Bank's connector so its contractor customers — like CoolAir HVAC — can get paid without leaving AirConPro's own interface. Before CoolAir HVAC can process payments, it goes through onboarding and underwriting as a sub-merchant of the bank's PayFac program — a one-time, business-level decision, walked through starting from the bank's own product offering page (**Flow 1**). Separately, at the moment of checkout on a specific job, Arnold Freeze can choose to pay by card or apply for financing through the bank — a transaction-level decision, distinct from CoolAir HVAC's own onboarding (**Flow 2**). Either way, the money lands directly on the bank's own ledger — a card payment settles, or the bank disburses a loan to CoolAir HVAC that Arnold repays over time — and the same confirmation view shows both types of money movement tying back to a single system of record.

**The scenario:** CoolAir HVAC gets started with ABC Community Bank's connector via the product offering page — logs in, sees prefilled information because they're already a depositor, and gets instantly approved (Flow 1). Later, a CoolAir HVAC technician closes a job inside AirConPro: Mr. Arnold Freeze needs a new central air conditioner installed, total price $8,000. At checkout, inside AirConPro's own software (not a bank-branded app), Arnold is offered two ways to pay: pay in full by card, or apply for financing through ABC Community Bank (e.g., $8,000 over 24 months) (Flow 2). Whichever path is chosen, the money still lands on the bank's own ledger and shows up in the same confirmation view.

**This should be more than an app — treat it like a small product launch package:** a product offering page (what the bank offers), an integration spec (how a partner plugs in), and a working proof of concept (the connector in action). That combination is closer to what a real Domain Owner would actually produce when introducing a new capability, and it demonstrates product thinking beyond just UI.

**Things to build, in priority order. Organized as two flows off a shared entry point — Flow 1 is the business/bank-relationship story (UX and the technical means to integrate it), Flow 2 is the consumer checkout experience, kept clean with no documentation interruption:**

1. **Product offering page (the entry point for both flows)** — a bank-branded screen (this is the one place Nymbus/ABC Community Bank branding and the full design system apply) presenting what ABC Community Bank offers SaaS partners: PayFac-as-a-Service (embedded payments) and Point-of-Sale Financing. Framed the way a bank would pitch this to a prospective integration partner like AirConPro. Includes a **"Get Started" call to action** that launches Flow 1. Keep it to one clean, well-designed screen — not a full marketing site.

**Flow 1 — Get Started / merchant onboarding, closing with the technical integration spec:**

2. From the product offering page, clicking "Get Started" leads CoolAir HVAC through: **(a) log into the bank** — a stylized login step only, not real authentication (stays consistent with the "no auth" constraint below — no real credential system, just a bank-branded login screen that proceeds on submit); **(b) prefilled merchant information** — because CoolAir HVAC is presumed to already be a depositor at ABC Community Bank, the bank already holds their business data on file, so the onboarding application shows up pre-filled rather than asking the user to type everything from scratch. This is a real, worth-stating product point: existing depositors get a frictionless embedded-payments upgrade, which is itself a reason for a business to bank there in the first place — ties directly back to the deposits thesis. **(c) Instant underwriting and approval** — this still calls the mocked "underwriting platform" stub (fake-working: brief realistic delay, stubbed decision, not real KYC/KYB), but because CoolAir HVAC is a known, existing depositor, the decision resolves quickly to Approved in this walkthrough. **The stub itself should still support a Denied outcome**, not just Approved — a real underwriting decision isn't guaranteed positive, and showing the demo can handle rejection is real edge-case thinking, even if the walkthrough itself shows the fast-approval path for an existing depositor.
3. **Integration spec (closes out Flow 1)** — a markdown document in the repo (not a UI screen, not an interactive API explorer) describing the connector API a SaaS platform like AirConPro would integrate against: endpoints for merchant onboarding/underwriting (what Flow 1 just demonstrated), transaction processing (card and financing branches — Flow 2's endpoints), and ledger confirmation, with example request/response payloads. This is the natural "here's how AirConPro's engineers would actually build against what you just saw" beat, positioned at the close of Flow 1 even though its content documents the full connector surface, including Flow 2. Write this *before* building the mocked endpoints — the stubs should implement a mocked version of what this spec describes, so the documentation and the code tell the same story instead of being two disconnected things.

**Flow 2 — Checkout and payment, purely consumer-driven, only reachable once CoolAir HVAC is Approved from Flow 1:**

4. **Checkout moment with two payment paths** — inside AirConPro's own UI (not the bank-branded page from Flow 1), the $8,000 air conditioner job is closed out and the homeowner is offered: (a) pay by card, or (b) apply for financing. This is the "payments in someone else's glass" moment, now enriched with a real choice.
5. **Card path** — use Stripe in test/sandbox mode, via Stripe Checkout or the Stripe Elements Payment Element, **themed with Stripe's Appearance API** so it visually blends into AirConPro's own UI rather than looking like a generic, obviously-Stripe-branded page. This is the fast way to get a real, working payment integration without building custom card-entry UI from scratch — pull styling from Stripe's own developer guide for the Appearance API rather than reinventing a payment form. **Note: an external API integration is optional per the actual assignment ("if it makes sense"), not a hard requirement — but it's worth including here because it's cheap to do this way and it's a real, working piece of the demo, not because it's mandatory.** Use one of Stripe's documented test-mode decline card numbers to show a real (not mocked) failed-payment state with a clear error message — free, and genuine error-handling evidence rather than only ever showing success.
6. **Financing path (mocked decision, same treatment as underwriting)** — if the homeowner applies for financing, call a separate mocked "financing decision" endpoint — fake-working the same way underwriting does (loading state, brief delay, stubbed approval with terms like amount/term/monthly payment). This is a transaction-level, consumer-facing decision, distinct from CoolAir HVAC's own onboarding in Flow 1 — don't conflate the two. **Same as underwriting: the stub should support a Denied outcome, with a clear message and a fallback nudge back to the card option**, not just an approval path.
7. **Funds landing on the bank's ledger + real-time confirmation view** — whichever path was taken, the money lands directly on ABC Community Bank's own core ledger: a settled card payment, or a disbursed loan. The confirmation view (the most important screen) shows AirConPro's transaction record next to the bank's core ledger entry, tagged by type (card settlement vs. financed disbursement), with a clear visual confirmation that they match because they're the same underlying event. One architecture, two kinds of money movement, always one system of record.
8. **A Kiro hook capturing session context automatically** — set this up early, not at the end. It should log prompts, decisions, and iterations as they happen, telling the story of the build in real time rather than a reconstructed summary written afterward. This is a required deliverable, separate from the app itself — easy to forget because it isn't part of "the product," but it's graded.

**Constraints:**
- Use these fictional names consistently: ABC Community Bank (the bank), AirConPro (the SaaS platform), CoolAir HVAC (the merchant/contractor). Nothing resembling a real institution or real HVAC software company.
- Keep AirConPro's UI thin — just enough to make the checkout moment feel real (a job/invoice screen is enough). Don't build a real field-service management app around it.
- Both the underwriting stub and the financing decision stub should fake-work: real-feeling API calls (loading state, brief delay, stubbed JSON response) rather than instant hardcoded statuses.
- Mock the bank-side ledger logic — no real banking integration, no real compliance logic.
- No authentication, no multi-tenancy, no admin tooling
- Stack: React + Next.js
- This is a scoped ~3 hour build. If time is genuinely tight, prioritize in this order: confirmation view (never cut) → integration spec (cheap, high signal, do it in writing even if the app build gets squeezed) → product offering page (cheap, one screen) → onboarding gate → checkout with both paths. The financing path can degrade to "shows its mocked decision and terms" without a fully wired second ledger entry if truly out of time.
- **Design system split, intentional:** the product offering page and all bank-side screens (onboarding status, ledger view, confirmation view) use the real Nymbus Design System (https://nymbus-joy.nymbus.com/design-system): DM Sans for headings/display, Inter for body/UI text, Geist Mono for code/data — a monospace treatment fits the ledger and confirmation views naturally. Primary action color is #2569EC (hover #1E54BD, pressed #163F8F), dark theme by default. AirConPro's side (including the themed Stripe checkout) should NOT carry Nymbus branding — it's a separate, unaffiliated software company in the story, and the bank being invisible to the end customer is part of the point.

**What success looks like:** someone unfamiliar with the concept should be able to read the product offering page, skim the integration spec, watch either a card payment or a financed purchase go through for CoolAir HVAC's $8,000 job, and land on the confirmation view understanding immediately why it matters — that because the bank was the payment facilitator and, where relevant, the lender from the start, there's nothing to reconcile, only something to confirm.

- **Product decisions and reasoning** — the PayFac-as-a-Service reframe (funds-native-to-the-bank vs. reconciliation-after-the-fact), why financing was added as a second transaction type, why underwriting and credit decisioning are deliberately stubbed rather than real.
- **What you'd change or add with more time** — seed this with something specific and real, not generic. Strong candidate: multi-bank optionality. A single-bank PayFac connector can't redirect funds if that one bank is frozen or fails (a real OCC concentration-risk concern for exactly this kind of platform) — a production version would need the connector to support routing across more than one sponsor/partner bank. Other real candidates: genuine KYB/ability-to-repay underwriting integration, event-driven (webhook-based) architecture for the connector instead of polling, real-time fraud scoring on the financing decision.
